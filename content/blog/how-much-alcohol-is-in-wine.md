## What is ABV?

ABV stands for **alcohol by volume**. It is the share of the liquid that is pure alcohol. A wine that is 13.5 percent ABV is 13.5 percent alcohol by volume. It is the standard way to state alcohol in wine, and it is a field on every HarvestLink bulk wine listing.

## How much alcohol does wine usually have?

| Type of wine | Typical ABV |
|---|---|
| Light, crisp whites and some sparkling wines | About 9 to 12 percent |
| Most still table wines | About 11 to 15 percent |
| Full-bodied reds from warm regions | Often 14 to 15 percent or higher |
| Fortified wines such as Port | Commonly about 18 to 20 percent |

These are typical ranges, not rules. Style, region, and vintage change them.

## What determines the alcohol in wine?

The main driver is **sugar in the grapes at harvest**. Yeast turn sugar into alcohol during fermentation, so the riper the grapes, the more alcohol the wine can have. Winemakers measure sugar in [brix](/blog/what-is-brix-in-winemaking) and estimate the potential alcohol by multiplying by about 0.55 to 0.65. Grapes at 24 brix, for example, can make a dry wine near 14 percent.

Other factors:

- **Climate.** Warmer regions ripen grapes faster and reach higher sugar.
- **Harvest timing.** Picking later usually means more sugar.
- **Yeast and fermentation.** Different yeast strains convert sugar at different efficiency, and a fermentation that stops early leaves sugar and less alcohol.
- **Additions.** In some cases winemakers adjust the level, subject to rules.

## How is alcohol measured?

Labs measure ABV with methods such as distillation or an instrument called an ebulliometer, and increasingly with spectroscopic analyzers. For a bulk wine purchase, ask for the lab's ABV on the actual lot, not an estimate.

## How exact does a wine label have to be?

Under the federal rule (27 CFR 4.36), the alcohol content on a wine label can differ from the actual level by a tolerance:

| Wine | Allowed tolerance |
|---|---|
| 14 percent ABV or less | 1.5 percentage points above or below |
| More than 14 percent ABV | 1 percentage point above or below |

For example, a wine labeled 12 percent can be anywhere from 10.5 to 13.5 percent in the bottle, as long as it fits the wine's other requirements. TTB also allows the words "table wine" or "light wine" in place of a stated ABV on wine at 14 percent or less. Wines above 14 percent must state their alcohol content.

This is a general summary, not legal advice. Confirm the rules for your wine with TTB.

## Why does ABV matter when you buy bulk wine?

- **Style and blend.** A high-alcohol lot can be balanced by blending with a lower one.
- **Label.** The number on your label has to match the wine within tolerance.
- **Taxes and class.** Alcohol level can affect the federal tax rate and category.
- **Price.** Buyers often filter by ABV first.

## What is potential alcohol vs actual alcohol?

**Potential alcohol** is what the grapes could produce if every bit of sugar ferments to dryness. **Actual alcohol** is what the finished wine contains. They differ when fermentation stops before all the sugar is used, so the wine is sweet and lower in alcohol, or when the winemaker makes adjustments.

## Is ABV the same as proof?

No. Proof is a term used for spirits. In the United States, proof is twice the ABV. Wine is described by ABV, and a 13 percent wine would be 26 proof, though nobody uses proof for wine.

## What about alcohol by weight?

Alcohol by weight is a different measurement. Alcohol is lighter than water, so a wine at 12 percent by volume is a little under 10 percent by weight. Wine labels and bulk wine listings use volume. If an old spec sheet or a foreign lab report uses another unit, ask for ABV.
