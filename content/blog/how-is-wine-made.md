## What are the basic steps of making wine?

1. Harvest ripe grapes
2. Sort, crush, and destem
3. Ferment the sugar into alcohol
4. Press and separate the wine from the skins
5. Age the wine in tanks or barrels
6. Clarify and stabilize
7. Blend
8. Bottle

Every winemaker adjusts these steps for the grape, the season, and the style they want, but nearly all wine follows this outline.

## Step 1: How do winemakers decide when to harvest?

Timing is one of the biggest decisions of the year. Winemakers watch several things:

- **Sugar**, measured in [brix](/blog/what-is-brix-in-winemaking), which predicts the alcohol
- **Acidity and pH**, which shape freshness and stability
- **Flavor and texture**, judged by tasting berries
- **Weather**, since rain or heat can force an early pick

Grapes are picked by hand or by machine, often at night or early morning to keep the fruit cool.

## Step 2: What happens at the crush pad?

Grapes are sorted to remove leaves and damaged fruit. They are then crushed, breaking the skins so juice flows, and usually destemmed. Red grapes go into fermenters with their skins. White grapes are typically pressed right away to separate the juice. The difference is explained in [red wine vs white wine](/blog/red-wine-vs-white-wine-how-they-are-made).

## Step 3: How does fermentation turn juice into wine?

Yeast eats the sugar in the juice and produces alcohol and carbon dioxide. That is fermentation. It usually takes one to a few weeks, and winemakers control the temperature because heat speeds it up and cooler temperatures preserve delicate aromas.

- **Native yeast** comes from the grapes and the winery.
- **Cultured yeast** is a selected strain added for predictable results.

When the yeast has consumed all or nearly all of the sugar, the wine is dry.

## Step 4: What is pressing?

Pressing squeezes the liquid out of the solids. In white winemaking it happens before fermentation. In red winemaking it happens after, when the wine is drained off and the skins are pressed. Gentle pressing produces cleaner wine but less volume.

## Step 5: What is malolactic fermentation?

After the main fermentation, many wines go through a second one, in which bacteria convert malic acid (sharp, like a green apple) into lactic acid (softer, like milk). It is almost standard for red wines and used selectively in whites.

## Step 6: How is wine aged?

The young wine rests in **stainless steel tanks** or **oak barrels**. Stainless steel keeps the wine fresh and neutral. Oak adds flavor and lets a tiny amount of oxygen in, which softens the wine. A standard barrel holds 225 liters, about 59 gallons. During aging, winemakers rack the wine, moving it off the sediment into a clean container, and taste it regularly.

## Step 7: How is wine clarified and stabilized?

Wine that is clear and stable in the bottle needs some help. Winemakers may fine the wine (add a substance that binds unwanted particles), filter it, and chill it so tartrate crystals form before bottling instead of after. They also check and adjust the level of sulfur dioxide, which protects the wine from spoilage and oxidation. See [sulfites in wine](/blog/sulfites-in-wine).

## Step 8: What is blending?

Winemakers often combine wines from different varieties, vineyards, or barrels to build the final wine. Blending can make a wine more balanced or more consistent from year to year. Lots that do not make the final blend are sometimes sold in bulk to other wineries and brands. See [what bulk wine is](/blog/what-is-bulk-wine).

## Step 9: What happens at bottling?

The finished wine is filtered if needed and put into bottles, sealed with a cork or another closure, and labeled. Some wines are released soon after. Others rest in bottle for months or years first. Before a wine goes on sale, its label must comply with rules, including the alcohol content statement. See [how much alcohol is in wine](/blog/how-much-alcohol-is-in-wine).

## How long does it take?

| Wine type | Typical time from harvest to release |
|---|---|
| Simple, fresh white or rosé | A few months |
| Oaked white | Six months to a year |
| Many reds | One to two years or more |
| Sparkling and long-aged wines | Several years |
